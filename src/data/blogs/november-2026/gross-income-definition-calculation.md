# Gross Income: What It Means and How to Calculate It

**Gross income is the amount you earn or receive before the deductions relevant to the calculation are taken out.** For an employee, gross pay is usually the amount shown before payroll taxes, insurance premiums, retirement contributions, and other withholdings. To calculate gross income for a period, add the income sources that belong in that calculation. For a simple salary-to-monthly conversion, divide annual salary by 12.

The phrase can mean different things on a pay stub, a tax return, a loan application, or a business worksheet. The useful first step is to identify what the form or decision is asking for. Gross income is not the same as take-home pay, and for a personal budget the amount that reaches your account is often the practical starting point.

## Gross income vs. net income

Gross income is before deductions. Net income is what remains after deductions. The FDIC’s Money Smart guide describes gross income as total income without deductions and net income, or take-home pay, as gross income minus deductions such as Social Security and income taxes ([FDIC Money Smart for Adults]([https://catalog.fdic.gov/catalog/sfc/servlet.shepherd/version/download/068t000000bhAKqAAM](https://catalog.fdic.gov/catalog/sfc/servlet.shepherd/version/download/068t000000bhAKqAAM))).

| Term | What it generally means | Example use |
|---|---|---|
| Gross pay or gross earnings | Pay before payroll deductions | Reading a pay stub or estimating monthly earnings |
| Net pay or take-home pay | Pay after deductions withheld from a paycheck | Planning what you can use from each paycheck |
| Adjusted gross income (AGI) | A tax calculation that starts with gross income and subtracts specified adjustments | US federal income-tax return calculations |
| Taxable income | The amount subject to tax under the applicable tax rules | Calculating tax owed |

These terms are related, but they are not interchangeable. In particular, AGI is not simply another name for take-home pay. Under US federal rules, the IRS describes AGI as total gross income from sources such as wages, tips, interest, dividends, and business income, less certain adjustments listed on Schedule 1 ([IRS AGI overview]([https://www.irs.gov/e-file-providers/definition-of-adjusted-gross-income](https://www.irs.gov/e-file-providers/definition-of-adjusted-gross-income))). Tax rules and definitions vary by location, so use the instructions for the specific form you are completing.

## How to calculate gross income

### If you earn a salary

For a steady annual salary, use the amount before deductions. To estimate average monthly gross pay, divide the annual salary by 12.

**Example:** If your annual salary is $60,000, average monthly gross pay is:

`$60,000 ÷ 12 = $5,000 per month`

That is an average over the year. It does not mean every paycheck is $5,000; your employer may pay weekly, every two weeks, twice a month, or monthly.

Pay frequency can make a monthly budget feel different from the annual average. With a $52,000 salary paid every two weeks, gross pay is `$52,000 ÷ 26 = $2,000 per paycheck`. The average monthly gross is `$52,000 ÷ 12 = $4,333.33`, but most months contain two paychecks, or $4,000 before deductions; two months in a typical 26-paycheck year contain a third paycheck. If you budget paycheck by paycheck, plan around the money that arrives in each month instead of assuming the average has already been deposited.

### If you are paid hourly

For a regular schedule, multiply your hourly rate by expected paid hours, then by the number of weeks in the period.

**Example:** At $24 per hour for 35 paid hours each week:

`$24 × 35 = $840 gross per week`

If you work 52 weeks at that schedule, the annualized estimate is `$840 × 52 = $43,680`. This is an estimate based on the stated hours and weeks. Overtime, unpaid time off, seasonal schedules, or changing shifts can make actual annual income different.

If hours change, use pay stubs or a schedule that reflects the period you need. For budgeting, you may want a conservative estimate based on income actually received rather than assuming every week will be identical.

### If you have bonuses, tips, or more than one job

Start with the gross pay from each source, then add the amounts that belong in the figure you are calculating. A simple personal-finance estimate might include wages from two jobs and a bonus expected during the year. Do not count the same amount twice—for example, adding a bonus to a yearly total that already includes it.

**Hypothetical example:** You earn $48,000 in salary and expect a $2,000 gross bonus during the year. The simple annual gross earnings total is `$48,000 + $2,000 = $50,000`, before payroll deductions. If the bonus is uncertain, keep it separate from the dependable monthly amount in a budget.

Whether interest, benefits, public payments, gifts, or other receipts count depends on the purpose of the calculation. A lender, government form, tax return, or budget may use different rules. Check the definition on the relevant application instead of assuming every incoming dollar is treated the same way.

### If you are self-employed

For a self-employed person, it is important to separate **business receipts** from **business profit**. Money collected by a business is not necessarily the owner’s personal income available to spend. The IRS’s US Schedule C instructions calculate net profit by subtracting eligible business expenses from business income; tax treatment and allowable deductions depend on the circumstances ([IRS Schedule C instructions]([https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1040sc](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1040sc))).

**Hypothetical example:** A freelancer receives $90,000 in business revenue and has $22,000 in business expenses. A simplified arithmetic difference is `$90,000 − $22,000 = $68,000` before personal taxes and subject to applicable accounting and tax rules. This example is not a tax calculation. It simply shows why gross receipts should not be confused with take-home money.

## A quick way to find the right gross-income number

Use this checklist before you calculate:

1. **Identify the period.** Is the form asking for hourly, weekly, monthly, or annual income?
2. **Find the pre-deduction amount.** For employment income, check the gross-pay line on a pay stub, not only the deposit amount.
3. **List the sources requested.** The form may ask for wages alone or income from several sources.
4. **Convert carefully.** For a regular paycheck, weekly pay × 52 gives an annualized estimate; biweekly pay × 26; twice-monthly pay × 24. Divide an annual amount by 12 for an average month.
5. **Label estimates.** Variable hours, tips, commissions, and seasonal work make an average a planning estimate, not a guaranteed amount.
6. **Use the form’s definition.** Tax, benefit, credit, and lending forms may define countable income differently.

## Which income should you use for a budget?

For a household spending plan, start with the money you can actually use: take-home pay after payroll deductions, plus other income you expect to receive. A budget based only on gross pay can leave too little money assigned to bills because taxes and benefits may already have reduced the deposited amount.

If your pay varies, estimate using recent pay stubs and keep uncertain bonuses or overtime separate. If you do not receive income monthly, you can convert a yearly estimate to a monthly average, then plan around the timing of deposits and bills. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s paycheck guide]([https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_building_block_activities_understanding-taxes-paycheck_guide.pdf](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_building_block_activities_understanding-taxes-paycheck_guide.pdf)) explains the distinction between gross income and the net amount received after deductions.

Once you know your usable income, list recurring bills, flexible spending, debt payments, and savings goals. Moneko’s [monthly budgeting guide]([https://www.moneko.io/solutions/budgeting-for-individuals/how-to-create-a-monthly-budget](https://www.moneko.io/solutions/budgeting-for-individuals/how-to-create-a-monthly-budget)) recommends building a plan from take-home income and recurring bills; it can be a useful next read if you are moving from an income calculation to a monthly plan.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is gross income before or after taxes?

For paycheck calculations, gross pay is before payroll taxes and other deductions. Net pay or take-home pay is the amount remaining after deductions. On tax forms, “gross income” can have a specific legal definition, so check the instructions for that form.

### How do I calculate gross monthly income from annual pay?

Divide annual gross pay by 12. For example, `$72,000 ÷ 12 = $6,000` average gross income per month. This is an average, not necessarily the amount of a particular paycheck.

### Is gross income the same as taxable income?

No. Taxable income is calculated under the applicable tax rules. For US federal income taxes, AGI is calculated from gross income minus certain adjustments; further steps determine taxable income. Other jurisdictions use different rules.

### Do self-employed people use gross revenue as personal income?

Not necessarily. Business revenue is before business costs. Business profit is calculated after applicable business expenses, and the amount used for taxes, lending, or budgeting can depend on the relevant rules and purpose. Keep business and personal figures distinct.

### Should I use gross or net income to make a budget?

Usually use net, take-home income for the money available to assign to spending and saving. If a budget tool or application specifically requests gross income, provide the figure it defines and use the requested period.
