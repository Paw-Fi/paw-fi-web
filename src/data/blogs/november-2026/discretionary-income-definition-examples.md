# What Is Discretionary Income? Definition and Examples

**Discretionary income is the money left after you cover essential living costs and required payments.** You can choose to save it, make extra debt payments, reserve it for a future goal, or spend some of it on things you want. **Discretionary spending** is the portion you actually use for optional purchases, such as dining out or entertainment.

For example, if you have $800 left after necessities and spend $200 on optional purchases, your discretionary income for that budget was $800 and your discretionary spending was $200. The other $600 may be saved or assigned another purpose. The [St. Louis Fed](https://www.stlouisfed.org/open-vault/2025/aug/primer-discretionary-income) makes the same distinction: money left after essential bills can be spent, saved, or invested.

Your exact figure depends on what your household needs to pay. A useful calculation starts with real bills and a clear definition, rather than a fixed percentage that may not suit your circumstances.

## Discretionary income and spending are different numbers

Discretionary income describes **what is available after essentials**. Discretionary spending describes **optional purchases you make**. Savings and extra debt payments are choices you can fund from the available amount, but they are not optional shopping.

Suppose your monthly take-home income is $3,600 and your essential and required costs total $2,800:

| Amount or action | Monthly figure | What it means |
|---|---:|---|
| Take-home income | $3,600 | Money available to budget |
| Essential and required costs | $2,800 | Bills and necessities included in this plan |
| Discretionary income | **$800** | $3,600 minus $2,800 |
| Optional purchases made | $200 | Discretionary spending |
| Amount left after those purchases | $600 | Available to save, reserve, repay debt, or use later |

The $600 is not “unspent income” you must use before the month ends. You can give it a purpose without making a purchase. If you transfer $300 to savings, for instance, that is a savings allocation. If you send $100 above a loan’s required payment, that is an optional extra debt payment. Neither is the same as the $200 spent on optional purchases.

This household definition is useful for budgeting, but it is not universal. The [US Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/cex/research_papers/garner-et-al-conceptual-framework-for-ce.htm) notes that discretionary income does not have one standard definition across all analyses. When comparing figures, check what each calculation includes.

## How to calculate discretionary income for a monthly budget

Use one consistent formula:

**Take-home income − essential and required costs = discretionary income for budgeting**

“Essential and required costs” is one combined group. Include each bill **once**, even if it is both necessary and contractually required. For example, a required car payment that you count under transportation does not need a second entry under debt payments.

### 1. Find the income you can use

Start with the income available for this budget period. For an employee, take-home pay is often a practical starting point because taxes and payroll deductions have already been taken from the paycheck. Add other income that is reasonably expected during the month.

If your income varies, avoid building the plan around your best month. Use an amount you can reasonably plan with, then revise the budget when more money arrives. Moneko’s [monthly budget guide](https://www.moneko.io/solutions/budgeting-for-individuals/how-to-create-a-monthly-budget) likewise starts with take-home income and suggests a realistic estimate when income changes.

If you start with gross income instead, account for the relevant taxes and deductions before applying the household formula. Do not subtract them again from take-home pay.

### 2. Add your essential and required costs

Common examples are housing, basic groceries, utilities, necessary transportation, insurance, healthcare, childcare needed for work, and minimum debt payments. What counts as essential can vary by household. Public transit may be enough for one person, while another needs a car to get to work.

Include predictable bills that arrive less often than monthly. If insurance costs $720 every six months, setting aside **$120 a month** accounts for it in your plan. Otherwise, the months without an insurance payment may appear to have more available money than they really do.

### 3. Subtract once, then plan the remainder

After adding the costs in your combined essential-and-required group, subtract that total from take-home income. The result shows what remains for your choices. You might decide that some should go to savings, an extra loan payment, a future expense, and optional spending.

If the result is negative, check for errors and timing differences first. Then look at costs you can change or payment arrangements you can discuss. Reclassifying a necessary bill as optional will change the label, but it will not create money to pay it.

## A worked household example

Consider a **hypothetical** person who has $4,200 in monthly take-home income. The table lists the costs they have decided are essential or required for their situation. The annual bill is known in advance, so they set aside one-twelfth of it each month.

| Monthly budget item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Take-home income | $4,200 |
| Housing | $1,500 |
| Basic groceries | $450 |
| Utilities | $180 |
| Transportation needed for work | $300 |
| Insurance | $220 |
| Necessary healthcare | $100 |
| Minimum debt payments | $250 |
| Set-aside for a $1,200 annual bill | $100 |
| **Total essential and required costs** | **$3,100** |
| **Discretionary income for this budget** | **$1,100** |

The calculation is **$4,200 − $3,100 = $1,100**. The $100 set-aside is included once because $1,200 divided by 12 months is $100.

Now suppose this person makes the following plan for that $1,100:

| Planned use | Amount | How to describe it |
|---|---:|---|
| Emergency savings | $400 | Saving |
| Payment above the required debt minimum | $250 | Optional extra debt payment |
| Money reserved for a future trip | $150 | Set aside, not yet spent |
| Dining out and entertainment purchases | $300 | Discretionary spending |
| **Total assigned** | **$1,100** | |

The four amounts add to $1,100. **Only the $300 used for optional purchases is discretionary spending in this example.** The person chose what to do with the rest of their available income, but saving or reserving money is different from buying something.

The categories are assumptions for this household, not rules for everyone. If the $1,200 annual bill were an optional membership rather than a required expense, the person could place its monthly set-aside among the choices made from discretionary income. They should still count it somewhere.

## Essential, fixed, and discretionary are not interchangeable

A bill can be **fixed** because its amount stays fairly predictable. That does not make it essential. A streaming subscription may cost the same every month but remain optional. Groceries are essential even though the amount varies.

A bill can also be required without every choice behind it being unavoidable. A car payment is due under a loan agreement; paying more than the required amount is a separate choice. Likewise, necessary transportation may be essential, while paying for a more expensive option could include a discretionary element.

| Expense | Likely treatment in a basic budget | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Rent or required mortgage payment | Essential and required | Include it once. |
| Basic groceries | Essential | The amount can vary from month to month. |
| Minimum loan payment | Required | An extra payment is a separate choice. |
| Streaming subscription | Usually discretionary | A recurring charge is not automatically essential. |
| Restaurant meal | Usually discretionary | It is separate from a basic food budget. |
| Savings contribution | Planned use of available income | Give it priority if that fits your goals; do not count it twice. |

These labels are tools for planning, not judgments about whether a purchase is worthwhile. Their purpose is to show which costs must be covered first and how much room remains for decisions. If you need help listing periodic household bills, Moneko’s [household expenses guide](https://www.moneko.io/solutions/budgeting-for-couples/household-expenses) includes recurring and irregular cost examples.

## Discretionary income versus disposable income

**Disposable income** generally means income after taxes. The [US Bureau of Economic Analysis](https://www.bea.gov/help/glossary/disposable-personal-income) defines disposable personal income as after-tax income available for spending or saving. For the household budget method in this article, discretionary income goes a step further: it subtracts essential and required costs.

Imagine $5,000 of income before taxes, $4,000 after taxes, and $3,000 in essential and required costs. In that simplified illustration, disposable income is $4,000 and discretionary income for budgeting is $1,000. Actual paychecks and formal income measures may include other details, so use the definition required by the form or decision in front of you.

## A note on US student loans

A US federal student-loan repayment plan may use **“discretionary income” as a program-defined figure**. It does not simply subtract your own rent, grocery bill, and insurance from take-home pay. Plan eligibility and payment calculations depend on official rules and can change. Use current [Federal Student Aid guidance](https://studentaid.gov/articles/faqs-idr-plan/) for a loan-program calculation; use your actual household costs for your personal budget.

## How to use the number each month

Calculate the amount after essentials before deciding what to spend on optional purchases. Check that irregular bills have a place in the plan, then choose amounts for savings, extra debt payments, future goals, and flexible spending. This makes a positive remainder useful without treating it as permission to spend all of it immediately.

Review the calculation when income or a major bill changes. If groceries regularly cost more than estimated, update the essential-cost figure. If a variable-income month brings in less than planned, adjust the choices you have not yet committed to. A budget becomes more useful when its categories reflect what actually happens.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does discretionary income have to be spent?

No. It is an amount available for choices after the costs included in your budget definition are covered. You can save it, reserve it for a future bill or goal, make an extra debt payment, or spend some of it.

### Is a savings contribution discretionary spending?

No in the framework used here. Moving money into savings is an allocation, while discretionary spending refers to optional purchases actually made. You can still decide to treat saving as a high-priority part of your monthly plan.

### Can a fixed expense be discretionary?

Yes. “Fixed” describes a relatively predictable amount; “discretionary” describes whether you can choose to make or continue the purchase. A monthly streaming subscription can be both fixed and discretionary.

### Why is my student-loan discretionary income different?

A US federal repayment program may apply its own formula based on its rules. Your household budget uses the actual essential and required costs you include. Check the applicable [Federal Student Aid guidance](https://studentaid.gov/articles/faqs-idr-plan/) for the program figure.
